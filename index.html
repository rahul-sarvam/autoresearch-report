SUMMARY

This study compares ways to combine recurrent memory, global attention and mixture-of-experts layers in language models.

The experiments began with a small-model architecture search and progressed through context extension, numerical repairs, optimizer studies and sparse-attention variants. The final campaign trained two fresh models of approximately 1.27 billion total parameters on the same 30 billion processed tokens. The aim was to identify a useful research baseline while measuring language prediction, retrieval, execution cost and numerical correctness separately.

The retained baseline is the A1 family, which alternates three recurrent KDA layers with one global MLA attention layer. In the final comparison, a HySparse2-style challenger performed better on some retrieval and context-extrapolation measurements, while A1 performed better on several language and copying measurements and trained faster. Both cached-decoding implementations failed the specified numerical checks. The study therefore retained A1 without establishing an overall architecture winner or a validated serving advantage.

In this repository, an epoch is a research campaign, rather than one pass through a dataset. The five campaigns use different scales and experimental setups. The sections below describe those changes, the evaluation procedures and the limits of the resulting comparisons.

00BACKGROUND AND METHODS

Study design and terminology

A language model predicts the next token from the preceding tokens. Tokens are pieces of text defined by a tokenizer; they are not necessarily whole words. During training, the model adjusts its numerical weights, or parameters, to improve those predictions. Model size describes the number of parameters, while a token budget describes how much training text is processed. Repeated text counts again toward the processed-token budget.

The context length is the number of token positions considered in an input. Here, 4K means 4,096 positions, 32K means 32,768, and 64K means 65,536. Training at a length and evaluating at that length are different conditions. Evaluation beyond the training length is called context extrapolation; successfully executing that evaluation does not establish useful retrieval or accurate generation.

Architecture components

A model layer typically combines a sequence mixer, which exchanges information across token positions, with a feed-forward network that transforms each token’s representation. This study changes the mixer, the feed-forward structure and parts of the training recipe in separate experiments.

Global causal attention lets each position read earlier positions. A query is compared with stored keys to obtain attention weights, which determine how the corresponding values are combined. Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA) compresses the information retained per token. It reduces the per-token representation, but its stored history still grows with sequence length.

Recurrent memory updates a fixed-size state as tokens arrive. It avoids retaining a separate global-attention entry for every earlier token, but must encode the relevant history within that state. The main recurrent component here is Kimi Delta Attention (KDA), which uses content-dependent decay and delta-rule updates. The decay controls how old state is retained; the update changes the state using the current input. The experiments test which kinds of information these mechanisms preserve.

Mixture of experts (MoE) changes the feed-forward network. In the tested four-expert, top-two recipe, a router selects two of four feed-forward parameter sets for each token. This is distinct from sparse attention, which selects earlier token positions. A model can have dense global attention and sparse expert routing at the same time. The number of active parameters per token therefore differs from the total number stored, and neither count alone measures computational work.

What the measurements mean

The figures report several distinct properties. A lower prediction loss does not imply that a model can recover a particular detail from a long input, and a successful retrieval probe does not establish general language quality.

Cross-entropy (CE)
and NLL
Negative log-likelihood measures how much probability the model assigns to the correct next token. The reported cross-entropy is the mean negative log-likelihood over scored targets, in nats: −mean(log p(correct token)). Lower is better. Raw CE excludes the MoE router-balancing penalty; some historical training objectives include that additional loss.
Copying and recall
A sequence appears, is separated by filler, and appears again. The test measures prediction of the repeated material. Historical “dense recall” is 1 − second-copy loss / first-copy loss, not a percentage of correct answers. Later plots report second-copy NLL or target-token hits directly. Under teacher forcing, earlier correct tokens are supplied to the model; errors are not fed back as they would be in free-running generation.
Passkey retrieval
A distinctive key is inserted into an input and requested near its end. Hit counts measure recovery of that key at particular lengths and insertion depths. This is a narrow synthetic retrieval test; it does not measure all of the reasoning or source selection needed for a long-document task.
Language tasks
Corpus loss measures token prediction on saved evaluation text. LAMBADA evaluates an exact continuation, and HellaSwag selects among candidate continuations. Their scoring rules and retained case counts are specified with the final results. Percentages from different tasks are not averaged into one model-quality score.
Execution and caching
Elapsed time and allocated GPU-hours describe the measured implementation and job scope. A separate cache check compares predictions obtained by reusing stored state with predictions from the full input history. Its numerical tolerances test equivalence; completing the process is not the same as passing that check.

Comparison controls

An optimizer uses gradients of the training objective to update parameters. The learning rate (LR) controls the update scale, so architecture comparisons also require a specified tuning budget. The early searches approximately matched non-embedding parameter counts by adjusting feed-forward width. Epoch 5 instead matched total parameters and processed tokens. These designs compare complete models under stated budgets; changes to depth, active computation or training history prevent treating every comparison as an isolated component ablation.

A training seed controls a training realization, including initialization. An evaluation seed can vary test inputs while keeping the trained checkpoint fixed. Most large endpoints in this study have one training seed. Multiple evaluation draws and numerical decision thresholds do not supply a training-seed uncertainty estimate. BF16 and FP32 denote 16-bit and 32-bit floating-point formats; the report specifies where storage or computation uses each because numerical precision also affects the recipe.

Historical extension and the final comparison are separate

The earlier campaigns trained selected models through 64K context and ran selected retrieval probes at 256K. The final A1/HySparse2 comparison trained only at 4K and evaluated through 32K. The historical results do not extend the validated scope of the final models. Each chapter identifies the checkpoints and conditions associated with its figures.

01EPOCH ONE / MIXER SEARCH

Epoch 1: mixer search and context extension

Full attention can revisit earlier tokens directly. Recurrent layers compress history into a fixed-size state. The initial search screened 26 mixer configurations, including recurrent, attention-based and hybrid designs.

The search selected one global attention layer after every three recurrent layers for further study. Kimi Delta Attention (KDA) supplies content-dependent memory; Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA) stores a compressed representation of each token. That 3:1 pattern became the backbone of A1.

TERMINOLOGY

“Dense A1” means dense global attention. Its recurrent layers and sparse expert routing remain part of the model.

Search scales and comparison controls

The first screen used models with roughly 85 million non-embedding parameters, then promoted promising combinations to 300 million and 1.2 billion. Each mixer received its own learning-rate search. At 300M, a rate selected using a short pilot was too low for the full six-billion-token comparison. Additional full-budget runs gave the compared architectures learning rates suited to that budget.

The experiments aimed to match parameter budgets within 0.5%. A mixer that needed more parameters left less room for the feed-forward network. Parameter matching therefore compared the combined mixer and feed-forward design. At 85M, adding a forget gate to MLA improved validation loss from 2.8320 to 2.7889 at the same analytic cache size. The gate changes attention scores according to distance; the 85M result supported including it in larger comparisons.

Context extension curriculum

The largest early runs first processed 30B tokens in 4K windows of FineWeb-Edu text. They then continued for 1.5B tokens at 32K and another 1.5B at 64K, using a 70/30 blend of PG19 books and FineWeb-Edu. The extension stages tested whether further training on longer windows improved use of earlier tokens.

At 300M, KDA’s dense-recall score across an 8,192-token gap rose from 0.213 → 0.531 → 0.678 through those stages. The test repeats a random 128-token sequence and measures how much easier it becomes to predict the second copy. It is not the percentage of tokens copied exactly. A separate passkey task asks the model to generate one planted key, testing a different kind of retrieval.

The measurements did not always rank models in the same order. Removing explicit position information or reducing the attention’s compressed-state size could preserve prediction loss while damaging a retrieval probe. A better short-context checkpoint also did not necessarily become a better model after extension. Epoch 1 selected the hybrid and curriculum for further study. The evidence did not establish a scale-independent architecture ranking. Historical models trained through 64K and selected 256K probes remain separate from the fresh 4K-trained models in the final chapter.

DECISION

The 3:1 KDA/MLA hybrid and staged context extension were selected as the baseline for larger comparisons.

02EPOCH TWO / KERNEL REPAIR AND CONTROLS

Epoch 2: Comba kernel repair and corrected comparisons

Comba is another fixed-state recurrent mixer, using coarser decay than KDA’s separate decay for each memory channel. It was promoted from the earlier search, but five 64K training attempts failed. Later analysis attributed those failures to a numerical defect in the chunked kernel: a normalized inner product exceeded its mathematical bound on a long run of repeated tokens.

Failure input and numerical mechanism

The crashes aligned with a PG19 region containing 360,448 consecutive copies of one token. In exact arithmetic, the normalized inner product cannot exceed one in magnitude. BF16 rounding produced values around 1.0026; repeatedly multiplying that small excess amplified the state until it overflowed at roughly 34K tokens. This explained how a 32K forward could succeed while the longer run failed.

Copying and passkey outcomes

Predicting an entire repeated random sequence asks the model to preserve many arbitrary details. Recovering one distinctive planted key asks a narrower question. Comba retrieved every key in the retained 256K trials while obtaining lower copying scores and higher text loss. KDA remained the reference for the next experiments. The repair removed the earlier evidence for an architecture-level training failure.

Corrections to intervention tests

One earlier ablation had suggested that Comba could lose any one of its three recurrent positions and still retrieve the key. Later auditing found that the intervention silently stopped running on most generation forwards as the prompt grew. Repairing it removed the apparent redundancy. Even the repaired block-splitting test changes convolution boundaries and kernel shapes as well as recurrent state; it cannot prove that recurrence alone caused the damage.

Other branches tested fixed decay rates, memory preconditioning, hierarchical storage and mixed decay granularity. None established a better overall replacement under the tested budgets: an intermediate copying improvement could disappear after further extension or come with worse passkey retrieval.

DECISION

KDA remained the reference; repaired Comba had stronger passkey results in the retained trials.

03EPOCH THREE / MOE EXTENSION

Epoch 3: attention gates under MoE

The forget gate preferred in the 85M dense comparison did not retain its advantage in the four-expert, top-two MoE recipe. During 32K extension, the forget-gate arm lost passkey retrieval at lengths it had previously handled.

The output-gate arm had better dense recall and passkey scores at the 64K-trained endpoint. The next baseline therefore used KDA × 3 + output-gated MLA.

GATE DEFINITIONS

A forget gate changes attention scores. An output gate scales the attention output. Neither is the same as the router that chooses experts.

MoE configuration and training controls

A dense feed-forward layer uses the same weights for every token. A mixture of experts, or MoE, instead trains several feed-forward networks and lets a router choose which ones process each token. Here each layer had four experts and selected two per token. The experiments approximately matched total non-embedding parameters, not active parameters or computation. The study examined how MoE affected the existing hybrid during context extension.

Both 1.2B MoE arms kept three KDA layers per global MLA layer and followed the 30B-token 4K stage, then 1.5B tokens each at 32K and 64K. They received the same four-point learning-rate screen and selected 0.00075. A rule suggested by the smaller MoE experiment—halve the dense model’s rate—did not transfer to this scale. The output-gate arm also had slightly more parameters: 0.49% more total and 1.91% more active, so the comparison was not perfectly isolated.

Retrieval during context extension

Before extension, the forget-gate model retrieved every tested key at 2K, 4K and 8K. After the 32K stage, it managed only 20% at 4K and zero at longer tested lengths. Its text objective and random-sequence copying did not show a comparable collapse. Continuing to 64K restored the short 4K key score, but not longer retrieval. The output-gate model finished with the stronger copying and passkey results shown above.

Attention probes measured probability assigned to the planted key span. After extension, the failing model assigned very little probability to that span, consistent with an addressing failure. The measurement did not isolate the training mechanism. Both MoE arms had imbalanced routing in the first layer, although only one lost retrieval. Neither that diagnostic nor attention mass alone established a complete mechanism.

Implementation and measurement controls

The MoE implementation required two corrections. Recomputing activations during training could lose the router-balancing gradient unless it was explicitly returned. Distributed workers could hang when they skipped different empty experts. Targeted controls exposed both problems and checked the fixes before the larger comparison.

The reported MoE validation objective also included the router-balancing penalty, whereas the dense reference reported language-model loss alone. Those numbers are not a clean measure of the prediction cost of MoE; later experiments logged the components separately. These single-seed results supported selecting output-gated MLA for the next campaign. The non-monotonic retrieval outcomes and failure mechanism remained unresolved.

DECISION

Output-gated MLA was selected for subsequent MoE experiments under this recipe.

04EPOCH FOUR / OPTIMIZATION AND SPARSE ATTENTION

Epoch 4: optimization and sparse attention

Epoch 4 evaluated optimizer settings, parameter precision and a learned sparse replacement for global MLA. The optimizer comparisons favored tuned HybridMuon in the tested settings. The final evaluation favored the retained A1 endpoint over sparse A2. Training objective and retrieval performance did not always improve together.

Training arms and comparison scope

A1 continued the original dense-attention model: 24 layers at width 1,024, with 18 KDA layers and six output-gated MLA layers, totaling 333,939,600 parameters. “Dense” describes those global attention layers; the feed-forward network still routes each token through two of four experts. A2 replaced the global layers’ full attention with learned sparse selection, training the model and its indexer together. A3 tested indexer-only adaptation; its final saved backbone tensors matched the parent, while six indexer projections changed.

A1 and A2 shared a nominal 1,430-update continuation plan. A3 had a shorter budget and a different indexer-loss weight, so it was not another equal-budget A2 trial. A2 also required a repaired recovery after failure. Repeated log coordinates and incomplete distributed RNG restoration prevent certifying equal actual processed work with A1. The final comparison therefore chooses between retained endpoints; it does not isolate sparsity as the sole cause.

Optimizer and learning-rate comparisons

HybridMuon applies Muon updates to eligible hidden weight matrices and AdamW to embeddings, routers and other parameters. Muon combines gradient momentum with approximate orthogonalization of matrix updates; AdamW uses running gradient statistics to scale updates. A one-training-seed, 1.5B-token bracket compared both optimizers on a shared learning-rate grid. The best AdamW objective was 2.7739 at LR 0.0015; Muon reached 2.7281 at 0.003, and a clipped canonical rerun reached 2.7265. These historical objectives include language-model loss plus MoE auxiliary loss.

The extension results showed a disagreement between objective and retrieval measurements. Muon at LR 0.0024 reached objective 2.6023 but scored zero on the historical 32K and 128K needle probes. LR 0.0012 reached 2.6835 while retaining 1.00 on both, and was selected. Those limited draws were not independent training repetitions or a retrieval guarantee. The retained recipe used gradient clipping before the update and monitored QK weight rescaling afterward; these operations are distinct from query/key normalization.

Parameter precision and memory use

Small updates could round away in BF16-stored parameters, particularly normalization gains and KDA decay parameters. FP32 storage enabled those updates, but the matched 381-update continuation did not show a quality gain: BF16 and FP32 ended at 2.6972 and 2.6969, comparable to their initial precision-dependent offset. Peak memory rose from 25.33 to 41.97 GB and throughput fell approximately 5%. BF16 remained the measured historical reference.

Implementation changes also affected training correctness and memory use. MoE auxiliary gradients had to survive activation checkpointing, and experts receiving no tokens had to remain connected to the distributed gradient calculation. Fixing checkpoint retention reduced a measured 1.2B/64K peak from roughly 110 to 33.3 GB. The memory reduction does not establish a model-quality improvement.

Sparse-selection correctness

The initial indexer could rank a block using information pooled from its future tokens. Masking the selected attention afterward could not undo that choice. Selection was corrected to compete only over earlier complete blocks, keeping the current partial block local; initialization and restart issues also required correction. Query tiling lowered memory, but did not by itself establish correctness. A separate saved A3 checkpoint remained sensitive to appended future tokens. That failure and A2’s later selected-row passes concern different checkpoints.

Shared copying evaluation

The final test froze three evaluation seeds, 4K and 16K contexts, and reference-present/reference-absent conditions. A 128-token reference appeared before filler and a gold copy prefix. The absent control replaced only the original reference, preserving filler, prefix and targets. Twelve cases scored 127 full-vocabulary targets each: 1,524 targets per endpoint. These seeds vary evaluation cases, not training runs.

The parent passed the prespecified mean criterion for benefit from reference presence at both lengths. One 16K seed produced only one hit out of 127. Teacher forcing supplies earlier correct tokens: “target hits” below measure next-token predictions on that shared history, not successful free-running copying.

A1 had lower loss and more target hits than A2 for every seed at both lengths; absent-reference hits were zero throughout. Independent scoring reproduced all 66 saved-array hashes, normalization and hits. Selected-row repeat and future-padding checks passed, without proving correctness at every position.

A2 was approximately 11.5 times slower at 4K and 32.6 times slower at 16K in these native timing samples. Gathered key/value tensors and selected-chunk execution imposed substantial overhead. The decision was to retain A1 and its tuned recipe, while preserving the sparse branch’s failures and the limits of the comparison.

EPOCH 4 DECISION

The campaign retained A1 with tuned HybridMuon and concluded the tested sparse branch. The archived results include its failures and recovery history.

05EPOCH FIVE / MATCHED TRAINING

Epoch 5: matched A1 and HySparse2 training

Epoch 5 compared the A1 family with a model that shares sparse attention across layers. The hypothesis was that retaining selected tokens from long histories could improve retrieval while preserving language-model quality. Both recipes started fresh at approximately 1.27B total parameters, with the same processed-token budget and an equal learning-rate search.

A1 contains 18 recurrent KDA layers and six output-gated dense MLA layers, arranged in the established 3:1 pattern. It has 1,269,005,856 total parameters, 24 layers and width 2,048. This is a new scaled model from the family, not a continuation of the historical 300M checkpoint.

The challenger follows the design in HySparse2: Hybrid Sparse Attention with Two-Level KV Sharing, with the implementation differences described below. The HySparse2-style model has 1,264,754,368 total parameters, 49 layers and width 1,024. Its 25-layer self decoder combines 24 local-window layers with one full-attention layer. The input to that full layer supplies independently projected keys and values to four cross-decoder groups. Each group contains one full-attention layer and five sparse layers that reuse its key/value (KV) representations and selected token indices, while computing their own attention weights. Selection retains a 128-token local window plus up to 1,024 nonlocal tokens, ranked by mean head probabilities.

Both models have four feed-forward experts with top-two routing; expert hidden widths are 1,312 for A1 and 1,792 for HySparse2. HySparse2 uses masked reference attention backends. It omits the paper’s mHC residual-connection mechanism, multi-token prediction and larger expert configuration. Total parameter counts differ by about 0.34%; depth, activated work, normalization and floating-point operation counts (FLOPs) are not matched. The experiment compares these complete recipes and cannot isolate the effect of KV sharing alone.

EVALUATION DEFINITIONS AND COVERAGE

Fixed-checkpoint evaluation

Each arm completed seven quality tasks in the fixed 4K suite and four in the 32K suite. Both cache-equivalence tasks failed.

The fixed evaluation scores the same cases for both terminal checkpoints. Raw CE uses 64 disjoint 4,096-target windows from each of FineWeb-Edu, PG19 and Book-QA: 262,144 targets per corpus. This measures token prediction on validation streams, including Book-QA as a text stream. It does not test question answering. These windows differ from training validation, so their absolute loss values are separate measurements.

Switch to “Downstream” for task accuracy. LAMBADA measures exact greedy continuation of the final word, choosing the highest-probability next token and allowing the gold word’s token count. HellaSwag selects among four endings using mean token log-probability. Each uses the first 1,000 frozen records, without skipping invalid rows. Their task definitions and units differ from corpus CE; compare the two models within each row.

HySparse2 has slightly lower FineWeb-Edu CE, 2.181562 versus 2.188247. A1 has lower PG19 and Book-QA CE, and more correct downstream cases: 470 versus 447 on LAMBADA and 450 versus 439 on HellaSwag. Synthetic passkeys favor HySparse2. The task ordering is mixed; averaging these measurements into a single score would require a choice of task weights.

CONTEXT EVALUATION PROTOCOL

Matched targets and forward shape

To measure the effect of additional history on a fixed prediction task, each window contributes the same final 256 target tokens at every visible-prefix length. The physical forward tensor length also stays fixed, addressing earlier observations that changing shape could change model arithmetic. Retained selected logits passed exact-repeat and future-padding intervention checks.

The separate 32K suite contains eight windows per corpus. Figure 6 varies the supplied prefix through 512, 2,048, 4,096 and 32,513 tokens, using 32,768-position forwards throughout. Scoring 256 next-token targets requires the prefix and the first 255 target tokens as inputs: 32,513 + 255 = 32,768. Shorter-prefix cases are padded after the scored positions to retain that physical shape. Its 4K marker denotes the training-window reference, not a change in execution shape. The shorter 4K suite used another 32 windows per corpus; its means must not be joined to this curve. Within that 4K cohort, both models improve from prefix 512 to 3,841, with larger endpoint reductions for A1 on all three corpora.

Both models first passed the fixed 8K → 16K → 32K structural admission sequence. Quality then ran once at the highest common accepted stage. Passing selected-row checks admitted the measurement; it did not guarantee good long-context predictions or arbitrary-position correctness. Neither model received extension training in this experiment.

At the longest prefix, HySparse2 has lower loss on all three corpora. Comparing 4,096 with 32,513 in each corpus shows that loss increases for both models. On FineWeb-Edu, A1 rises from 1.917607 to 2.181511 nats, while HySparse2 rises from 1.934794 to 1.969698. The advantage is smaller extrapolation degradation, not a monotonic benefit from adding history. Windows are slices of consecutive token files at fixed offsets, rather than document-aligned examples; a slice can join the end of one document to the start of the next. These small retained cohorts do not establish population-wide behavior or unseen-source generalization.

The passkey task tests retrieval of a distinctive five-digit key planted in filler. The key is placed near the beginning, middle or end, at depths 0.1, 0.5 and 0.9. Each cell below contains five trials. The 4K suite uses 512-, 2,048- and 4,091-token prompts; the 32K suite uses 32,763-token prompts. Generation reruns the full token history at every step; the score is a substring match within six generated tokens.

A1 retrieves 40/45 keys in the 4K suite and 10/15 at 32K; HySparse2 retrieves 45/45 and 15/15. The position breakdown is specific: A1’s five 4K misses all occur at the earliest depth of the 4,091-token prompts, while its five 32K misses occur at the latest depth. Perfect performance on these fixed cases is not a guarantee for other keys, texts or reasoning tasks.

The copying diagnostic presents a 128-token chunk, filler and the chunk again. It scores the same 127 targets in the first and second copies, with 16 trials per gap. Natural chunks come from PG19; random chunks use synthetic token IDs. Figure 8 shows second-copy negative log-likelihood (NLL) under teacher forcing: earlier gold tokens are supplied, so the metric measures prediction loss rather than free-running copy accuracy.

At the 32,512-token gap, HySparse2 has lower natural-copy NLL, 1.975 versus 2.533, while A1 has lower random-copy NLL, 9.627 versus 10.310. Both still assign higher probability to the repeat than to the first copy. At the separate 4K gaps, A1 has lower loss in every natural-copy comparison; HySparse2 has lower random-copy loss at the longest gap. The ordering depends on input type and distance.

At the same token budget and GPU count, the saved training-attempt timers give a 2.27× elapsed-time ratio. This is an observation about the implemented recipes, including their different batching and attention paths. The measurement does not cover optimized sparse kernels or isolate one architectural component. Allocated GPU-hours describe reserved hardware time, without measuring utilization.

Serving requires a separate test because cached decoding follows another execution path. A 2,048-token prefill builds the state; 32 subsequent single-token appends reuse it. At every step, the evaluator compares predicted logits—the unnormalized scores used to form token probabilities—with a fresh full-history forward on identical supplied tokens. A1 uses stock KDA recurrence plus an alternate eager FP32 MLA readout; HySparse2 uses its native cache API.

Acceptance checks every compared logit using |cached − reference| ≤ 0.01 + 0.01 × |reference|. The largest absolute difference summarizes the mismatch, but is not the complete pass/fail rule: the relative term depends on the reference value.

These failures leave cached serving unvalidated, while the full-forward quality measurements remain separately recorded. Retained cache sizes and instrumented timings cannot establish an accepted serving-speed or memory-quality advantage on paths that fail equivalence. Earlier tiny cache passes did not transfer to the trained endpoints. No tolerance was widened to obtain a pass, and the saved receipts do not localize the cause of the mismatch.

INTERPRETATION AND LIMITS

Conclusions

The project retained the A1 family as its research reference: three KDA recurrent layers per output-gated global MLA layer, with four feed-forward experts and top-two routing. The earlier campaigns supported that choice under their tested budgets. They also showed that learning-rate selection, numerical repairs and evaluation definitions could change the interpretation of an architecture comparison.

The final matched run did not support replacing A1 with the tested HySparse2-style recipe. Quality was mixed across tasks, HySparse2 required more recorded training time, and cached serving remained unvalidated for both models. HySparse2’s passkey results and smaller degradation on the longest matched-target prefixes remain measured advantages on those cohorts. Retaining A1 is an endpoint decision, not a claim that it is uniformly better.

Limits of the comparison

There is one final training seed per arm and a bounded learning-rate search. The models differ in depth, active work, attention implementation and physical batching. The fixed evaluation cases and repeated probes describe these trained endpoints; they do not establish population-level significance or identify one architectural feature as the cause of a difference.

Source identity and evaluation independence also require separate evidence. Saved hashes bind a result to particular inputs and checkpoints, but cannot establish that evaluation documents were absent from training. Earlier source checks were partial; Book-QA provenance and exposure questions remained unresolved. The reported corpus scores therefore do not establish unseen-source generalization.

The study does not validate transfer to 100B- or 1T-parameter models, production serving at 128K or 1M context, or the performance of optimized sparse kernels that were not implemented. Historical 64K-trained checkpoints and the fresh 4K-trained final models retain their separate scopes. The two trained cache failures remain unresolved.

Evidence and reproducibility

The article uses the corrected epoch reports and the saved final evidence archive. Figures plot recorded measurements; connecting lines do not add observations between them. Training curves retain all 116 validation points per arm, and the downloadable dataset also includes sampled, unsmoothed training observations. No training or model evaluation was rerun for this publication.

The archive records training logs, evaluation procedures, per-case results, checkpoint hashes and scheduler observations. Its verification establishes file identity and recorded bindings. It is not an independent rerun of the experiments. Large checkpoints, corpora and some saved arrays remain external to the repository.
