THE THROUGH LINE

The best loss, the best retrieval, and the cheapest implementation were rarely the same model. This is the story of how five rounds of experiments made that distinction clearer.

Real runsCorrected conclusionsOpen questions retained
01EPOCH ONE / FINDING THE PATTERN

A little attention.
A lot of memory.

Full attention can revisit earlier tokens directly. Recurrent layers compress history into a fixed-size state. The search began with 26 mixer configurations and a question: how much global attention does a hybrid actually need?

One attention layer after every three recurrent layers offered a useful compromise. Kimi Delta Attention (KDA) supplies content-dependent memory; Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA) stores a compressed representation of each token. That 3:1 pattern became the backbone of A1.

A NECESSARY DISTINCTION

“Dense A1” means dense global attention. Its recurrent layers and sparse expert routing remain part of the model.

CARRIED FORWARD

A hybrid pattern worth scaling. Small-model rankings remained provisional.

02EPOCH TWO / REPAIRING THE EXPLANATION

The architecture
didn’t cause the crash.

Comba, the closest recurrent challenger, had failed in five 64K training attempts. The initial architecture-level interpretation was wrong. A numerical defect in the chunked kernel let a normalized inner product exceed its mathematical bound on a long run of repeated tokens.

03EPOCH THREE / ADDING EXPERTS

A small gate.
A different outcome.

The gate that looked promising in the small dense model did not carry its advantage into the four-expert, top-two MoE recipe. During 32K extension, the forget-gate arm lost passkey retrieval at lengths it had previously handled.

The output-gate arm retained much more retrieval. The difference motivated the next baseline: KDA × 3 + output-gated MLA.

TWO DIFFERENT GATES

A forget gate changes attention scores. An output gate scales the attention output. Neither is the same as the router that chooses experts.

CARRIED FORWARD

Use the output gate for the next MoE experiments. A useful recipe decision, not a universal rule.

04EPOCH FOUR / TESTING THE ALTERNATIVES

Keep the dense layer.
Tune the recipe.

The next campaign tested optimizer choices, precision and a learned sparse replacement for global MLA. HybridMuon helped at its tuned learning rate. But a lower training objective could still conceal collapsed retrieval, and FP32 storage did not demonstrate a quality gain in the short matched continuation.

The final comparison favored dense A1 over sparse A2 on every retained seed at both tested lengths. A2 also cost substantially more in the measured implementation.

CARRIED FORWARD

Retain A1 and the tuned optimizer. Stop the tested sparse branch; preserve its failures.

05EPOCH FIVE / THE MATCHED TRIAL

Same budget.
Different strengths.

For the final experiment, both recipes started fresh. Approximately 1.27B total parameters. The same FineWeb-Edu token stream. The same 30B processed-token schedule. Equal three-point learning-rate searches before the main runs.

A1’s 24-layer hybrid faced a 49-layer HySparse2-style model that shares global key/value representations and selected token indices. This matches total model size and training budget, not depth, activated work or FLOPs.

READING THE ENDPOINTS

There is no single winning column.

A1 leads several language measurements. HySparse2 does better on selected retrieval and extrapolation probes.

WHAT WE KEEP

A reference.
A record.
Better questions.

Retain the A1 family as the research reference. Preserve the HySparse2 endpoint and its gains. Promote neither result into a universal architecture claim.

The lasting contribution is the evidence: completed runs, corrected explanations, fixed evaluation instruments, and failures that stayed visible.
